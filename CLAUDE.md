# canvas-particles-space

A WebGL particle flight through space, ending in a black hole. React only drives the UI. The scene itself runs in one requestAnimationFrame loop.

## UX

The main quality of the interface is the balance between flexibility (the user can customise layout and look) and carefully chosen defaults for both, so every panel reads well before anyone touches it. Readability, compactness and clarity for the user are the main goal of UX: every UI change is judged by them first.

- Microcopy is one short line. A control's hint fits one line of the 340px panel (at most 42 characters): what the control changes, in plain words, with the direction when it is not obvious ("Higher: shorter trails"). A paragraph under a slider (several sentences, physics, edge cases, references to other settings) is an anti-pattern: it breaks compactness and nobody reads it. The full explanation stays in the spec's `description`, which the agent API reads; the context menu shows the spec's `hint`.
- The panels draw no hint lines and no help buttons. A hint lives in the control's context menu (`hint_menu.tsx`, one menu for the page): a right-click, Shift+F10 or the Menu key, or a long press on touch opens it with the control's name, its hint and, where it has a default, a reset to it. A row offers its hint through `data-hint`, `data-hint-label` and `data-hint-reset`, and keeps the same text as an `sr-only` line tied to its control with `aria-describedby`, so screen readers always read it. Do not add per-row help icons or a HELP toggle.
- A panel opens and closes from one place: its button in the scene toolbar at the top right (`control_panel.tsx`), which never moves or hides and shows the panel open with `aria-expanded` and a solid fill. Panels have no close buttons of their own. Opening moves the focus into the panel; Escape closes the panel the focus is in; closing with the focus inside returns it to the panel's button.
- A slider's label and value never sit on a strong fill or a blend mode: the fill is a quiet tint with a solid edge at the value. Every value carries its unit, formatted once in the spec (`format` in `simulation_specs.ts`, `unit` in `INTRO_PARAM_SPECS`) and spoken through `aria-valuetext`.
- Scrolling never changes a value: the wheel moves a slider only while it has the focus or with Shift or Alt, and a finger moves it only after a sideways slop or with a short tap, never one that lands while the panel scrolls.

## Render performance

The hard constraint in this project is frame time. Any change that touches the **per-frame path** has to keep it lean. The per-frame path is:
- `renderFrame` in `src/components/particle_canvas.tsx`
- `advance` in `src/lib/scene.ts`
- `stepParticles` in `src/lib/particle_simulation.ts`
- `render` in `src/lib/particle_renderer.ts`
- the composite fragment shader in `src/lib/webgl_shaders.ts`

The bottleneck is the **main thread**, not the GPU. About 88% of main-thread time is the `stepParticles` **hot loop**, at roughly 50 ns per particle. Any per-particle cost is multiplied by the particle count, 24 000 by default and 12 000 on small screens.

### Frame pipeline

1. `renderFrame` reads both stores with `getState()`. It applies the frame-rate cap through the `nextFrameSlot` scheduler, derives `frameScale` from the real elapsed time and hands both stores to the scene as its `SceneInput`. `advance` (`src/lib/scene.ts`) assembles the frame from that input alone.
2. `stepParticles` advances a struct-of-arrays field (Float32Array per property) and writes clipped segments into one interleaved instance array: 7 floats per segment, with the colour stored as a packed Uint32.
3. `render` uploads the instances with a single `bufferSubData` (a film's crew leaves them as runs, which go up one after another) and draws instanced quads into a persistent scene FBO that holds the trails. It then runs one full-screen composite pass, which does the lens, the intro effects and the title, and draws the space sheet over it while that is on.

### Invariants

- **The scene reads no store, no window and no clock.** `createScene` owns the field, the black hole, the intro frame and the renderer; what a frame depends on arrives in `SceneInput` (settings, intro parameters, hidden layers, reduced motion, particle count) with `frameScale` and `dt`. The page keeps the loop, the frame-rate cap, the particle budget, input handling and every store write. Random numbers come from the scene's own seeded generator (`src/lib/random.ts`), never `Math.random`, so one seed and one sequence of inputs draw the same frames; that is what lets a film render the scene in a plain loop.
- **The per-frame path allocates nothing.** `frame`, `settings`, `instances` and `composite` are created once with the scene, the page's `input` once in the effect, and all are mutated in place. New per-frame data becomes fields on these objects or preallocated typed arrays, not fresh objects, arrays or closures.
- **React never renders per frame.** The loop reads stores through `getState()`. It writes to a store only when a value changes, and only at a throttled rate:
  - `measuredFps`: every 500 ms;
  - `activeParticles`: when the budget changes it;
  - the debugger readout: every 66 ms, and only while the debugger is open.

  Every `set()` also wakes every subscriber, including `syncLoop` in the canvas.
- **Fast-changing values stay in leaf components.** A component that shows `readout`, `measuredFps` or `activeParticles` subscribes to just that value through a narrow selector. Examples are `MeasuredFps` and, in the debugger, `TimeReadout`, `Ruler`, `Playhead`, `TrackValue`, `SpanBar` and `MarkerButton`. Large components such as `IntroDebugger`, `IntroTrackList`, `TrackRow` and `ControlPanel` never subscribe to them, so they do not re-render at loop rate.
- **The hot loop rejects cheaply before it computes.** It compares squared distances first, because the event horizon captures particles at any depth, then applies a scalar range test (the depth band `holeDepthNear..holeDepthFar`) before the gravity math. It uses polynomial falloffs instead of `Math.exp` or `Math.pow`, at most one `Math.sqrt` per particle on the gravity path (one more for a particle within reach of a second black hole, while there is one), and multiplications by precomputed reciprocals. Loop-invariant values are hoisted above the loop. Respawning goes through `spawnParticle`; a captured particle goes through `respawnBeyondHorizon`, which keeps a wide horizon from swallowing the whole field.
- **The particle count follows the adaptive budget.** `src/lib/particle_budget.ts` trims the count when the simulation takes more than 40% of the frame and restores it once there is room again. The field count follows `particleBudget.active` everywhere, `restartIntro` included, not `numParticles`. Users can turn the budget off with the Particle Budget switch in the Playback section (ON/OFF).
- **Motion is frame-rate independent.** Per-frame motion is scaled by `frameScale`, which is relative to a 60 Hz reference and capped at 6. Seconds-based motion uses `dt`. `src/lib/frame_pacing.ts` treats a one-off stall as a normal frame, so a long task on the main thread does not make the particles jump. Trails fade in whole 60 Hz steps through `fadeBacklog`. That keeps trail length the same at any refresh rate and avoids the grey 8-bit haze that per-frame fading leaves on an RGBA8 target. A particle is a filled disc that sweeps a round-capped capsule over a frame. It is stamped into the scene with the brighter-of-two blend (`MAX`), not added, so overlapping stamps of a slow particle do not build up and its edge stays sharp at any size; its brightness comes straight from its speed per 60 Hz frame in the particle vertex shader, and the trail is the fade of the scene; the Speed Glow setting (`u_speedGlow`) bends that link, and `u_frameScale` keeps it the same at any refresh rate. A particle that did not move writes no stroke, so at Base Speed 0 the field goes dark except for the particles the black hole's gravity moves; gravity must not be scaled by the flight speed.
- **The composite shader gates every effect behind a uniform.** It runs for every device pixel on every frame, so each effect sits behind `if (u_effect > 0.0)` and its uniform is 0 while the effect is inactive. After the intro, only the lens, the shadow, the ring and the background stars do any work. A new effect follows the same pattern.
- **Opening Settings builds nothing.** `SettingsBody` is built once with the page, before the first frame, in a detached element that the open panel attaches. A long task while the scene runs makes the next frame jump, so do not move this work back to the moment the panel opens. A closed section stays mounted and is only hidden, so expanding one builds nothing either; every toggle is a leaf component bound to its own setting. Each slider subscribes to its own setting through `SettingSlider`, so changing a setting re-renders one slider, and `settings_persistence.ts` writes to the browser in idle time.
- **The space grid costs the main thread a few numbers per frame.** The Lensed Grid is one block of the composite shader behind `u_skyGrid`, read at the bent ray like the stars. The Sheet (`src/lib/space_grid.ts`) is a static buffer of line pieces that the grid vertex shader places, dents and ripples; one instanced draw lays it over the finished frame, and only while Sheet is above 0. `placeGrid` finds where the well goes; nothing about the sheet is computed per line in JavaScript. The well has a meaning (`dent` in the grid vertex shader): a place is lowered by how much time is slowed where the light seen at that distance passed closest to the hole, so the sheet is flat far away and Well Depth deep at the shadow's edge, where that light skims the photon sphere.
- **A second black hole costs nothing while its size is 0.** `stepPair` (`src/lib/companion.ts`) turns the pair around the place the black hole stands, by Kepler's law for their masses, grows the main hole as their shadows overlap and marks the moment they become one; it is a few numbers per frame. The hot loop takes the companion's capture and pull in one block behind `hasCompanion`. In the composite shader its lens (a point mass added to the main lens), its shadow, its ring and the pair's spiral gravitational wave each sit behind `u_companion.z` or `u_pairWave.x`; the sheet gets a second well and the same wave from uniforms, with nothing computed per line. The merger is the pair's own motion, not the intro's: `stepPair` gives the merged hole's tone (`ringPitch`, `ringLength`, from the spin of what is left) and the angle and sense the pair had when it merged, and `advance` makes of them an oval that turns as it rings (`u_holeOval`, two numbers) and a wave that goes on from the spiral at the spiral's strength, on the pair's speed of light (`LIGHT_SPEED`) and at the rate of Orbit Speed. That wave is drawn by the intro's wave block, which is why the block has a centre of its own (`u_gravityWaveCenter`), a turn (`u_gravityWaveTurn`) and a front where the spiral ends (`u_pairFront`); the opening's and a fired ringdown stand still and keep the intro's light speed and multipliers. Every gravitational wave here moves the picture across its path, never along it, and weakens as 1 over the distance (`waveReach`); a new wave follows both.
- **Uniforms go through the dirty-checked setters** `setUniform1f`, `setUniform2f` and `setUniform4f` in the renderer, which skip unchanged values. The texture units are fixed: the scene on 0, the title on 1, the photon ring's energy (`src/lib/ring_energy.ts`) on 2, the orbiting stars' traces on 3.
- **The camera's zoom redraws, it does not magnify.** A zoom above 1 (the Zoom setting, the intro's camera) is applied to the geometry: the particle and orbit vertex shaders place their strokes at the zoom (`u_zoom`), so the scene layers already hold the magnified picture, and the composite pass reads them through `u_layerZoom`, the zoom they were last drawn at. In that pass every edge and hairline is measured in `px`, the scene pixels under one screen pixel (the shadow's edge, the ring's 1.5 px floor, the lensed grid's lines, the background stars' size), so nothing softens or thickens with the zoom. A new shape in the composite shader follows the same rule. A camera pulled back below 1 is left to the composite pass. The particles' trails are what the camera saw: a stroke starts where the particle stood in the last frame's picture (`u_zoomFrom`), so a moving camera streaks them and a jump of the camera (`CAMERA_CUT_ZOOM`, `CAMERA_CUT_SHIFT`) starts them afresh. The title is a sprite and the intro's dust is noise; those two still soften when magnified.
- **Orbiting stars leave lines, not a picture.** `stepOrbiters` (`src/lib/orbiters.ts`) integrates at most 64 stars with real velocity and hands the renderer one row a frame: the stretch each star is flying, in Schwarzschild radii from the black hole. A row is flown for `STRETCH_SECONDS` and then a new one begins, whatever the frame rate. The renderer keeps the rows in a ring on the GPU (one `bufferSubData` of at most 64 records a frame, room for every star's longest trace, made when the first star is drawn) and draws the whole layer anew each frame through the orbit program, around the hole as it is now and at the camera's zoom, so the traces follow the hole, grow with it, outlast a resize and stay sharp at any zoom. A stretch's age is kept as the brightness the traces had lost when it was flown (`dimmed`), which the vertex shader subtracts, so a trace fades evenly to black and a trail that changes length dims what is there at the new rate. The composite pass reads the layer only while `u_orbits` is 1.
- **GPU buffers grow and are reused.** The instance buffer grows ×1.5 through `bufferData` and is otherwise refilled with `bufferSubData`. The resolution follows `PIXEL_BUDGET` (4.5 M device pixels), with `MAX_PIXEL_RATIO` 2.
- **Resize is coalesced into one rAF.** World coordinates do not depend on the viewport, so a resize reallocates only GPU targets and keeps the particle field.
- **The loop runs only while it is visible.** `syncLoop` stops it while the scene is paused, while the tab is hidden and while the WebGL context is lost. Every GL resource is created inside `createParticleRenderer`, so `webglcontextrestored` rebuilds it all. A new texture or buffer belongs there.
- **The scene needs WebGL2 and has no fallback.** A browser without it gets the message in `particle_canvas.tsx` instead of the scene, so the renderer calls WebGL2 directly and no feature gets a second code path. The shaders are still GLSL ES 1.00, which WebGL2 compiles as it is.

### Measuring

A change to the per-frame path is done only when its before/after numbers are in hand and the main-thread time per frame does not exceed the baseline beyond noise. Runs vary by about ±10%, so take two runs of each build. If a regression is the price of a visual feature, report it in numbers rather than hiding it.

- **Throughput.** Serve two production builds (`pnpm build`, then `dist/`): the commit before your change and your change. Load each in headless Chrome with `--use-angle=d3d11 --ignore-gpu-blocklist --disable-gpu-vsync --disable-frame-rate-limit` at 1920×1080. Click CLICK TO START, wait 7 s for the intro to finish, then count rAF ticks over 4 s. Read CDP `Performance.getMetrics` before and after, and divide the `TaskDuration` delta by the frame count to get main-thread ms per frame. To find where the time goes, use a CDP `Profiler` capture with self time per function.
- **Simulation.** Bundle a microbenchmark that calls `stepParticles` directly (esbuild ships in `node_modules/.pnpm`). Run 300 warm-up steps and then 1500 timed steps at 1920×1080. Measure 24 000 particles with the hole, and 24 000 and 10 000 without it.

Baseline at e9c18fc, measured with the method above:

| Scene | FPS | Main thread | Simulation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Default | 590–650 | 1.5–1.7 ms/frame | — |
| `?debug`, debugger open | 575–585 | 1.7 ms/frame | — |
| `stepParticles`, 24 000 with hole | — | — | ~1.45 ms/step |

## Driving the scene as an agent

`window.space` (`src/lib/scene_api.ts`) reads and changes every simulation setting and intro parameter and drives the intro timeline. It is loaded in development, and on a deployed build only with `?agent` in the address, as a separate chunk that other visitors never download.

How an agent uses it is the `space-scene` skill (`.claude/skills/space-scene/`): driving the page, making films, the map of every knob by the law it belongs to (`references/knobs.md`), film craft (`references/films.md`), the MCP tools from a shell (`scripts/space.mjs`) and the audit of what agents reach (`scripts/audit.mjs`). Each fact has one home, so nothing is written twice: ranges and meanings are in the specs and read through `describe`, the page's commands in `help()`, the film format in `FILM_GUIDE`, what belongs together and how to use it in the skill, the rules for the code here.

The browser comes from the `chrome-devtools` MCP server in `.mcp.json` ([chrome-devtools-mcp](https://github.com/ChromeDevTools/chrome-devtools-mcp)). It opens its own Chrome window with a persistent profile, separate from the user's browser, so the agent's changes never touch the user's saved settings. Its flags keep the scene rendering while the window is covered or in the background.

- **An agent from outside finds the door in four places, none of them in the UI**, all fed by one text, `public/index.md`. The page's own HTML carries it as plain markup inside `#root`, where the scene will stand (the `describePage` plugin in `vite.config.ts` puts it there at build, from the few kinds of markdown the file uses), which is what an agent reads that fetches the address as text and runs no script; a `<meta name="description">` says the same in one sentence. It must be markup, not a `<noscript>`: a chat assistant given the link read nothing from a `<noscript>`, and Mozilla Readability, which such tools use, throws that element away (0 lines before, the whole text after). React replaces it on mount, and a class the page sets on `<html>` before the body is parsed keeps a browser that runs scripts from ever showing it; with scripts off it is the page. A request for the page that asks for markdown (`Accept: text/markdown`) is answered with `public/index.md` instead of the page: a route in `vercel.json`, because a rewrite gives way to the `index.html` that exists at that address. The page's response names that file in a `Link` header as its `service-doc`. The canvas carries a label for the accessibility tree (`SCENE_LABEL` in `particle_canvas.tsx`). `index.md` names commands and leaves every range to `describe()`, so change it only when a command it names changes. Preview deployments are behind Vercel's sign-in: check a change to the routes with the project's bypass secret in an `x-vercel-protection-bypass` header.
- A setting or parameter is described once: simulation sliders in `src/lib/simulation_specs.ts` (the Settings panel builds from it), intro parameters in `INTRO_PARAM_SPECS`. A new setting goes there, and both the panel and the agent API pick it up.
- Every length around the black hole is in black hole radii (Event Horizon, Gravity Reach, Einstein Radius, Orbit Size, the second black hole's Size and Distance), so Black Hole Size alone scales the hole and nothing has to be kept in step with it. A new such setting follows the same rule. `preset.apply('Schwarzschild')` is the physically faithful setup (`SCHWARZSCHILD_PRESET` in `src/store/preset_store.ts`): a point-mass lens with its mirrored image inside the Einstein ring, no twist, capture at the shadow's edge. Default is tuned for the look instead.
- `set` and `intro.set` check every value, put it on its slider's step within range and apply the whole patch in one store update; an unknown key fails with the closest names. `reset` and `intro.reset` bring back the defaults.
- **A change to the knobs ends with the audit.** After a setting, an intro parameter, a layer or a preset is added, renamed or removed, `node .claude/skills/space-scene/scripts/audit.mjs` must print no problem: it loads the registries and checks that `set` takes every key, that the scene reads no setting an agent cannot write, that a film takes every setting and moves every number, switch and colour along a track, and that the skill's map names every key and none that is gone.

## Films

A film is the scene as a 1-180 s mp4, described by one JSON object: frame and length, seed, starting settings, the intro as a clip, keyframes for any numeric setting or intro parameter, captions. The format is written once, as `FILM_GUIDE` in `src/lib/film.ts`, and the script that is a shorter way to write it as `SCRIPT_GUIDE` in `src/lib/film_script.ts`; `window.space.film.guide()` and the `space_guide` tool both return the two, so change the guide when the format or the language changes.

- **The `space` MCP server** (`mcp/space_mcp.mjs`, in `.mcp.json`) is how an agent makes one: `space_guide`, `space_describe`, `space_check` (the film read back as a timeline, without drawing), `space_preview` (a contact sheet of frames; the storyboard of a film cut into shots) and `space_render` (the mp4, with an optional audio file under it). Each takes a film as its JSON (`film`) or as a script (`script`, with `input`). It serves `dist/` from this machine and keeps one headless Chrome on it, so run `pnpm build` after changing the page. It has no dependencies: JSON-RPC lines on stdio, the browser over its DevTools socket. The page does the work through `window.space.film` (`check`, `preview`, `render`, `progress`, `cancel`), which also works from the console of any page with `?agent`.
- **A film plays in a scene of its own** (`src/lib/film_render.ts`): a second `createScene` on a canvas of the film's size, stepped by a fixed `dt` with no clock, so the page's scene and its saved settings are never touched and a slow machine draws the same frames as a fast one. It starts from the page's defaults, never from this browser's settings. Motion is stepped at about 60 Hz for a film of up to 60 fps (`frame.present` is false for the steps between two frames) and once a frame above that, up to 240 fps: the same motion at any rate, though not the same frames. The same seed gives the same frames; a preview frame is the frame the render makes.
- **`planFilm` checks a film against the same registries as `window.space`** (`src/lib/agent_values.ts`): a new simulation setting or intro parameter is settable and animatable in films as soon as it is in its spec. A setting that is not a slider needs a line in `parseSettings`, and one in `readTracks` to move along a track: a switch flips at its key's second (`TRACK_SWITCHES`), the palette is set and never moved. A film with several mistakes is told all of them at once: each part is read through `attempt`, which keeps the problem and reads on, so a new reader fails through it and never stops the check at the first. What a film asks for and will not get is a note, not silence (the opening's film look without `filmHold`, a face without the words' letters).
- **A film is a timeline, and each ability of the scene is one kind of thing on it**: a value moves along keyframes (`tracks`), a layer switches (`layers`), the intro is a clip (`intro`), an event of the intro is fired again (`cues`), and the scene's own time slows or stops (`speed`). What the intro debugger does with time and layers, a film does, and the audit holds that. A layer is declared once in `src/lib/intro_tracks.ts`: its line in `INTRO_LAYER_NOTES`, and its place in `CUE_LAYERS` if it is an event; `describe`, the guide and the checks read that. `scene.fire` plays events on three clocks kept with the scene (the blast, the ringing, a title), sampling the intro's own timeline into a second frame and laying it over the first, so a fired event is drawn by the code that draws the opening and costs nothing while none plays; a film with no speed and no cues takes the path it took before and its frames are the same to the bit. A fired title can carry words of its own: they are drawn before the first frame (`scene.makeTitle`) and put in place at the cue (`scene.useTitle`), since a film cannot wait for a face in the middle. The opening's film look (letterbox, vignette, grain, fringe) leaves with the opening; the intro parameter `filmHold` is the share of it that stays. `speed` bends the scene's step, never the film's seconds: the intro's start is worked out in scene time from it (`introTime`), and slow motion takes finer steps. `planFilm` returns the film as it understood it (`timeline`), which `check` and every preview show.
- **A film written as a script is the same JSON, said shorter** (`src/lib/film_script.ts`): JavaScript that plays the film forward on a cursor of time (`wait`, `to`, `drive`, `shot`, `caption`, `fire`, `meanwhile`), so no second is written by hand, a part of a film is a function and the film is as long as what was played. `writeFilm` runs the text in the page and returns the film object `planFilm` takes; `check`, `preview` and `render` take a string as a script. Nothing reaches the renderer but through that JSON, so a new ability goes into the film format first and then gets its word. The words are `SCRIPT_WORDS`, each told in `SCRIPT_GUIDE`. A value's moves are gathered and written as keys when the script ends (`writeKeys`): a film's own ease is one key, a spring or a function is 60 linear keys a second (`BAKE_RATE`), and two moves of one value at once are refused with both lines. A script's chance comes from the film's seed, `Math.random` included, so the same script is the same film. A mistake names its line, found on the stack through the script's `sourceURL`; a mistake of grammar gets its line in the MCP server (`vm.Script`, which reads and never runs), because the page's engine does not say it. The audit holds that the guide tells every word and its example runs, that a script and the JSON it stands for give the same plan, that a script moves everything a film moves, and that the lines are named.
- **Two things of a film are not the scene's**: `fade`, a share of black laid over the finished frame and under the captions (`filmFade`, drawn in `film_render.ts`), and `shots`, names for stretches of the film that change no frame and make a preview a storyboard, one frame from the middle of each. A film without them takes the path it took before.
- **Captions are drawn over the finished frame** on a 2D canvas (`src/lib/film_captions.ts`), never in the scene: the page has no captions. A line wider than the frame is broken at its spaces (`fitCaption`, worked out once for a caption), and words in a face that lacks their letters go to the bundled face that has them (`faceFor` in `film.ts`: of the display faces Cyrillic is in Unbounded alone), because a fallback to the machine's own face would make the same film a different video elsewhere.
- **A caption's text is what popular science is written in** (`src/lib/caption_text.ts`, read once by `planFilm`): a mark is a sign with a brace right after it, so plain text with an underscore or an asterisk of its own is never read as one (`_{s}` lowered, `^{2}` raised, `*{term}` in the accent colour, `#{key:1}` a number read from the film at that second: the caption's own `values`, the film's `seconds`, or any number of the scene after the tracks). Behind every bundled face stand Noto Sans, Noto Sans Math and Noto Sans Symbols (`public/fonts`, declared in `index.css` each with only the characters the faces before it lack, so a caption loads no file it will not use and the page none at all), which hold Greek, mathematics and the signs of the Sun and the planets; `COVERED` in `caption_text.ts` is every character those files hold, read from their character maps, and is what the note about the machine's own face goes by, so a new font file means a new `COVERED`. A line with no marks that is all there is drawn as the one string it always was, which is what keeps films without the new things the same to the bit; a line with marks, a caption that arrives a letter, a word or a line at a time (`reveal`), and one with `align` are drawn piece by piece from one layout (`layOut`). A caption with a live number is laid out again when its words change and keeps the block of its widest number (`steady`), so nothing shakes as digits change.
- **A film is encoded by WebCodecs** through `mediabunny`, imported only when a film is rendered, so visitors never download it. The bitrate is counted for 60 fps at most (`BITRATE_FPS`): the browser's software H.264 encoder, which is what takes 4K and anything above 120 fps on this machine, gives each frame a 60 fps frame's bits whatever the rate and refuses a budget above about 300 Mbit/s. That encoder names H.264 level 5.2 at most, so a stream above its named level gets the true one written into the decoder's description (`raiseAvcLevel`).
- **A render through the MCP server is never held in the page.** The file is sent as it is encoded, a piece at a time with the place the piece belongs at (`upload`, `at`), and the server writes each piece in place; room for the index is kept at the start of the file (`fastStart: 'reserve'`, which needs the count of packets of every track), so the file plays before it is all there and its size is not bound by the tab's memory. Only a film saved by hand with `download` is put together in the page.
- **A film's particles are stepped on several cores, and the frames stay the same to the bit** (`src/lib/particle_crew.ts`). `stepParticles` is `stepRange` over the whole field; a crew cuts the field into pieces that the main thread and up to seven workers step with that same loop (`particle_pieces.ts`). What reaches beyond one particle is written down and played back on the main thread in particle order (`StepLater`, `settleLater`): the respawns, which draw from the scene's one random sequence, and the light for the photon ring. So a new random draw, sum or counter in the hot loop goes through `StepLater`, and a new numeric step setting goes into `STEP_NUMBERS` (the build fails without it). The pieces' segments are not packed together, because copying within shared memory cost as much as the step itself; the renderer uploads the runs in order (`instanceRuns`). A crew needs memory shared between threads, which a page gets only with the two isolation headers the film server sends; on the deployed page and the dev server a film is stepped by the plain loop. Check a change to the step by comparing a preview's bytes between the film server and a server without the headers.
- **What a film costs** (`graph_paper.json`, 38 s at 1080p30, 8 cores): a preview to its last second took 4.2–4.5 s with the plain loop and takes 1.4–1.5 s with the crew, 1.26 ms a frame; a render took 6.2–6.5 s and takes 5.7–6.1 s. A render is held by the video encoder, which Chrome already runs on the graphics card, at about 200 frames a second at 1080p, with the main thread busy 1.7 ms of each 5 ms frame. Measured and of no use: encoding from the WebGL canvas without the 2D copy, `prefer-hardware`, `realtime`, reading the pixels back by hand, other H.264 profiles. Two hardware encoders side by side reach about 300 frames a second, at the price of a second of frames held in video memory; that is not built.
- Verify a film change by rendering: `space_preview` twice with one seed must give the same picture, and an mp4 must play (`<video>` in Chrome, or mediabunny's `Input` in Node for its tracks).

## Fractal films

A fractal film is the film renderer forked for another picture: a zoom into the Mandelbrot set, to any depth, described by one JSON object whose format is `FRACTAL_GUIDE` in `src/lib/fractal.ts` (`window.space.fractal.guide()`, the `space_fractal_guide` tool). It touches none of the scene and none of the per-frame path.

- **It shares the film's machinery and nothing else.** `encodeVideo`, `exclusively`, the progress, the cancel and the contact sheet (`framesAt`, `sheetLayout`, `createSheet`) are in `film_render.ts` and serve both renderers. A change there is checked by both: particle films' previews stay the same bytes, a fractal film still renders.
- **The target is exact, the pixels are not.** `fractal_orbit.ts` places a Misiurewicz point by Newton's method on BigInt fixed-point numbers, to the bits the deepest frame needs plus `GUARD_BITS`. Only the target's orbit reaches the GPU, as float32 pairs in a texture: a Misiurewicz point's preperiod and one turn of its cycle, which the shader loops over. Any other point is stored as its BigInt orbit up to where it escapes.
- **A pixel is perturbation with an exponent of its own** (`fractal_shaders.ts`, GLSL ES 3.00): δ and dz/dc are a mantissa and an exponent, the step at the orbit's first entry (where Z is 0) gives each term its own exponent, and a pixel whose orbit comes nearer 0 than the reference, or reaches the end of an ending orbit, is rebased to the first entry. That is what keeps one reference free of glitches at any depth. A point target and the Misiurewicz target at the same place must draw the same frames, which is how a change to this is checked.
- **A frame is a function of its second.** `fractalView` gives the pixel size, the turn, the colours' phase and the steps a pixel takes (`stepsToReach` on the orbit's growth of |dz/dc|), so a preview draws only its frames and two previews are the same bytes. A loop zooms whole copies of the picture (the cycle's multiplier), turns back by their angle, shifts the palette by their steps and keeps one step budget, so its last frame leads into its first.
- **A frame is drawn in bands of rows,** each waited for with a fence and sized to about `BAND_MS`, so a heavy frame never holds the GPU long enough for the system to reset it. A 1080p frame with 2×2 samples took about 100 ms at ×10³⁰ on an RTX 3070.
